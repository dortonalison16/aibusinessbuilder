Start Here
You're already spending hours on your phone. This guide helps you turn some of that time, and the things you already know, into income you control.

Let me tell you where this guide comes from. The founder of Grit & Bloom started out in a work-from-home healthcare job at $19 an hour, handling insurance authorizations and benefits. It was "flexible" on paper. In reality, being idle for even five minutes earned a "ding" in the system, and the paycheck never felt like it matched the cost of being watched all day while her kids were in the next room.

She was a teen mom with no college degree. What changed things wasn't a big break. It was one online course, one small side project, and a willingness to keep building. That led to a role running client success for online businesses, then years inside the operations teams of well-known, multimillion-dollar online companies, fixing what was broken and streamlining what wasn't working. Over four years, she's helped more than a thousand business owners get their own offers off the ground. Today she's the mom of five, and she runs operations for a multimillion-dollar company.

She's telling you this for one reason: you don't need a degree, a big following, or a perfect plan to start. You need a clear next step. That's what this guide is.

Who this is for

This guide is written for the mom who's working a 9–5, or trying to, and wants income she controls so she can be home more. If you've ever thought "I'd love to leave, but I can't just quit, and I don't even know what I'd sell," you're exactly who this was made for.

What you'll be able to do by the end

  • Pull together what you already know and see what's worth packaging.
  • Choose one simple digital product and write the promise that makes people want it.
  • Set up a page, checkout and automatic delivery so it sells while you're at work or asleep.
  • Create faceless content that sends people to it, with no camera and no dancing.
  • Follow a 14-day plan from idea to a live product.
How to use this guide

Don't read it all in one night. Read a chapter, do the small action at the end, and move on. Each chapter builds on the one before it. If you do only the actions, you'll have a product by the end.

An honest note

This guide teaches a method. It doesn't promise a specific income. Results depend on your effort, your product and your market, and most first products take a few rounds of testing before they sell steadily. Anyone who promises overnight income is selling you something. We'd rather give you a real plan.

Chapter One

Find What You Can Package

You know more than you think. Let's get it out of your head.

The number one thing that stops women from starting is "I don't have anything to sell." That's almost never true. You have a job, a home, a family, a history, and a list of problems you've already solved. Somebody a step or two behind you would pay to skip the hard parts.

You don't have to be the world's best expert. You have to be one step ahead of the person you're helping. That's enough.

The 10-minute brain dump

Set a timer for ten minutes and write down answers to the prompts below. Don't edit and don't judge. Bad ideas are welcome, because they often sit right next to the good one.

PromptExamples to jog your memory
Jobs I've hadEvery role, even the ones you think don't count: retail, healthcare, admin, customer service, managing a team
Things people ask me for help withBudgeting, organizing, spreadsheets, picking a school, meal planning, writing emails, resumes
Problems I've solved for myselfGetting out of debt, working from home with kids around, negotiating a raise, staying organized
Things I do without thinking that others find hardPlanning a party, learning software fast, staying calm in chaos, explaining things simply
Big life lessonsStarting over, being a young mom, leaving a bad situation, building confidence, going back to work
What I've paid to learnCourses, books, coaches, tools. The lessons you've already bought and tested

Turn the list into product ideas

Now look at your list through three filters. Circle anything that passes at least two:

  • Painful: does it solve a problem someone actively wants gone, like a headache, a time-waster, or a fear?
  • Specific: can you say who it's for in a sentence? "Moms" is too broad. "New moms going back to work who feel behind" is a person.
  • You can back it up: have you done it, or helped someone do it? Even one real example is enough.
Your first product doesn't need to be your best work. It needs to help one specific person get one specific result.

Four kinds of products that work for beginners

TypeWhat it isWhy it's good to start with
Guide or playbookA short PDF that walks someone through getting a result step by stepFastest to make, easiest to sell, easy to update
Templates or swipe filesReady-to-use documents, planners, trackers, scripts or checklistsPeople love skipping the blank page, and you make it once
Mini course or workbookA few short lessons or worksheets, in a PDF or a simple set of videosFeels more valuable and supports a higher price
Resource bundleA curated pack that saves someone hours, like prompts, scripts or a toolkitGreat as an add-on or a follow-up product
Use AI to speed this up

Paste your brain dump into an AI assistant and ask: "Based on this, what are five digital products I could create for [your audience]? For each one, name the problem it solves and who it's for." You'll get ideas you'd never see on your own. You decide which one feels right.

Your action for Chapter 1
Do the 10-minute brain dump using all six prompts.
Circle your top three ideas using the painful, specific and can-back-it-up filters.
Ask two people in your life: "What do you always come to me for?"
Chapter Two

Choose Your First Product

One simple product, one clear promise, one price.

You have three ideas circled. Now we pick one. The goal isn't the perfect choice. It's the fastest useful one, because getting a first product live teaches you more than another month of thinking.

The 3-question test

Score each of your three ideas from 1 to 5 on these questions, then add them up:

  1. Would someone pay to fix this? Not "like it," but pay. Think of a time you saw people asking about it or complaining about it.
  2. Can I make a useful first version in a weekend or two? If it takes months, it's too big for a first product.
  3. Do I enjoy talking about it? You'll be creating content about it for a while. Pick something you don't mind repeating.

The highest score wins. If it's a tie, choose the one you can finish fastest.

Write your one-sentence promise

A good product promise is short and finishes a sentence like this:

The promise formula

Helps [specific person] go from [frustrating before] to [desired after] using [your simple method], without [the thing they fear].

A few examples so you can see how it reads:

  • Helps new moms returning to work go from feeling behind and overwhelmed to running a calm morning routine, using a 20-minute weekly reset, without buying a planner system.
  • Helps home-based moms go from scrambling for dinner to a stress-free week, using a ready-made menu and shopping list, without spending Sundays in the kitchen.
  • Helps first-time freelancers go from guessing what to charge to sending confident quotes, using a plug-and-play pricing template, without a spreadsheet degree.

Set your price

For a first digital product, most people land between $17 and $47. Here's how to think about it:

PriceWorks well forWatch out for
$9–$17Small templates, checklists, add-onsHard to earn much unless you sell a lot
$27–$47Guides, playbooks, workbooks. The sweet spot for a first productNeeds a clear promise and a good page
$67–$97+Mini courses with real depth and supportNeeds more trust, proof and polish
Don't under-price out of fear

A very low price can make people think it isn't good. Pick a price where a buyer would say yes without much thought, and where you'd feel proud of what they receive. You can adjust it later.

Name it

The best names say what the product does. Clever names are fun, but clear names sell. If you can add a short subtitle that finishes the promise, do it: "The Sunday Reset: a 20-minute weekly plan for calmer mornings."

Your action for Chapter 2
Score your three ideas with the 3-question test and pick a winner.
Write your one-sentence promise using the formula.
Choose a price and a working name. You can change both later.
Chapter Three

Build It: Page, Checkout & Delivery

Set it up once so it sells and delivers without you.

Here's the good news: a digital product doesn't need a fancy website or a developer. It needs three simple pieces that work together, and once they're set up, the whole thing runs by itself.

The three pieces

PieceWhat it doesWhere it lives
1. The productThe thing they receive: a PDF, templates, videos or a workbookA file you upload once
2. The pageExplains the promise and convinces them to buyA simple one-page site on your selling platform
3. Checkout & deliveryTakes payment, then instantly emails the download linkBuilt into most selling platforms

Step 1: Make the product

Keep the first version small and useful. A 15–30 page PDF that gets one clear result beats a 200-page book nobody finishes. A simple structure that works almost every time:

  1. The promise: what they'll be able to do by the end.
  2. The quick win: something they can do in the first 15 minutes.
  3. The steps: 4–6 short chapters, each ending in a small action.
  4. The tools: checklists, templates or examples they can use right away.
  5. The next step: what to do after finishing.
Let AI do the heavy lifting

Give an AI assistant your promise, your audience and your brain dump, then ask it for an outline and a first draft of each chapter. Edit it so it sounds like you and includes your real examples. Then use a free design tool to lay it out. The draft is a starting point, and your experience is what makes it worth paying for.

Step 2: Choose where to sell

Pick one beginner-friendly platform that handles the page, the payment and the delivery in one place. Look for these features:

  • Instant automatic delivery of the file after payment
  • A simple, mobile-friendly sales page
  • Order bumps and upsells (you'll want these later)
  • Email collection, or an easy way to connect an email tool
  • Reasonable fees. Check the platform's current pricing before you commit.

Step 3: Write a page that sells

A sales page is just a conversation on a page. Follow this order and you'll cover what a buyer needs:

SectionWhat to say
HeadlineYour promise, in plain words. Who it's for and the result they get.
The problemDescribe their frustration so well they think "that's me."
The solutionIntroduce your product and how it works, in a few short lines.
What's insideA simple list of what they'll get, with a benefit for each.
Why youA short, honest bit about why you can help. Your story goes here.
The offerPrice, what's included, and a clear button.
QuestionsAnswer 4–5 common doubts: "Is this for me?" "How long will it take?" "What if I get stuck?"
Only promise what you can back up

Don't make income claims or guarantees you can't prove. Describe the result your product genuinely helps with. Trust is your most valuable asset. It's also easier to keep than to rebuild.

Step 4: Test it like a buyer

Before you tell anyone, go through the whole thing yourself:

  • Buy your own product (most platforms let you use a test mode or a 100% discount code).
  • Check that the delivery email arrives and the download link works on your phone.
  • Read your page on a phone. Most of your buyers will see it there.
Your action for Chapter 3
Write a first draft of your product using the 5-part structure.
Pick one platform and upload your product.
Build your page with the 7 sections, then test-buy it.
Chapter Four

Faceless Content That Sells

Get seen without ever being on camera.

A product nobody sees doesn't sell. Content is how people find you, and the good news is that you don't need your face, your voice, or a big following to do it well. Plenty of successful brands are built on text, graphics and simple screen recordings.

Five faceless content types

TypeWhat it looks likeGood for
Text-on-screen reelsShort video with words appearing over calm footage or a colored background, with musicReach and quick tips
CarouselsSwipeable slides, one idea per slide, with a hook on slide oneTeaching and saves
Screen-record tutorialsRecording your screen while you show a tool or process, with optional voiceoverTrust and demos
Quote and story graphicsA single strong statement or mini-story on a branded graphicConnection
Voiceover with visualsAn AI or recorded voice over stock footage or slidesExplaining bigger ideas

Your five content pillars

Rotate through these so you never run out of ideas:

  1. Teach: one small tip that helps them today.
  2. Relate: "if you're a mom stuck in a 9–5, you'll get this." Name their exact frustration.
  3. Show: behind the scenes of your process (no face needed: screens, notes, results).
  4. Prove: results, feedback, or small wins from you or your customers, once you have them.
  5. Invite: tell them clearly about your product and where to get it.
Most of your content should help. A little should sell. But every piece should point somewhere.

Hook formulas to steal

The first line decides whether anyone keeps reading. Fill in the blanks:

  • "If you're a mom with a 9–5 and you want [result], start here."
  • "I went from [before] to [after]. Here's the first thing I did."
  • "Stop doing [common mistake]. Do this instead."
  • "[Number] things I wish I knew before I started [thing]."
  • "You don't need [thing they think they need]. You need [simple truth]."

A weekly rhythm that fits real life

Consistency beats intensity. This is a plan you can actually keep with work and kids:

WhenWhatTime needed
Once a weekBatch-create 3–5 pieces: write the hooks, design the graphics or slides, and record any screen tutorials60–90 minutes
Once a weekSchedule them all for the week using a scheduling tool15 minutes
DailyReply to comments and messages5–10 minutes
Every post needs one next step

End with a single, clear action: follow, save, comment a keyword, or click the link in your bio. Don't leave people wondering what to do. Point them to a free item or straight to your product.

Be patient with the numbers

New accounts usually grow slowly at first. Give it a fair run of at least 30 days of consistent posting before you judge it, and pay attention to which posts get saved and shared. Do more of those.

Your action for Chapter 4
Pick one platform to start on (you can add more later).
Write 5 hooks using the formulas above, one for each pillar.
Create and schedule your first week of content.
Chapter Five

Affiliate Links as a Side Income

Recommend what you already use and earn when others sign up.

Affiliate marketing is simple: you recommend a tool or product, and if someone signs up through your special link, the company pays you a commission. You don't make, ship or support anything. It's not a replacement for your own product, but it's a useful second income stream that grows alongside your content.

Where it fits

Your audience is already learning to build a small online business. That means they'll need tools, and you're using some of them already. Recommending tools you genuinely use is natural, helpful and honest.

What they needToolWhat it's good for
Selling and delivering your productSysteme.ioSales page, checkout, automatic delivery and email in one place
Link-in-bio storeStan StoreOne simple link that sends your content audience to your product
Building a communitySkoolA home for your people, with classes and discussions
Designing contentYour favorite design toolGraphics, carousels and simple video
Scheduling postsYour favorite schedulerBatch your week in one sitting

Disclosure: some links in this guide are affiliate links. If you sign up through them, Grit & Bloom may earn a commission at no extra cost to you. We only recommend tools we would use ourselves.

The four rules

  1. Only recommend what you truly use. If you wouldn't tell a friend, don't post it.
  2. Always disclose. Tell people when a link is an affiliate link, and check the disclosure rules that apply where you and your audience live. In the U.S., the FTC requires clear disclosure.
  3. Read each program's terms. Some restrict how you can promote them, for example in paid ads or with certain wording. Rates and terms change, so check the current details.
  4. Keep the list short. Three or four tools you can talk about honestly beat a long list of links.
Don't let it distract you

Affiliate income is a side dish. Your own product is the main meal, because you keep the whole sale and you build something that's yours. Add affiliate links after your first product is live, not before.

Your action for Chapter 5
List 3 tools you already use and would recommend.
Check whether each has an affiliate program and read its terms.
Add a short, honest disclosure wherever you share a link.
Chapter Six

Your Next Step

What to do once you're live, and how to grow from here.

Once your first product is live, the work shifts from building to learning. Your job for the next 30 days is simple: get it in front of people and pay attention to what happens.

What to watch

What you noticeWhat it usually meansWhat to try
Nobody visits your pageNot enough people are seeing your contentPost more consistently, and test new hooks
People visit but don't buyThe page or the promise isn't clear enoughRewrite the headline, add answers to common doubts
A few people buyYou have something. It's workingAsk them what they liked, and use their words in your page

Add small offers

Once you have buyers, the easiest way to earn more per sale is to add small extras at checkout: a companion template pack, a bonus checklist, or a quick-start bundle. Buyers are already saying yes, and a well-matched add-on often gets a yes too.
