# Ad Psychology, Storytelling & the Pull Strategy

Read this before writing ads. It's the difference between copy that gets scrolled past and copy
that gets screenshotted and sent to a friend.

---

## 1. Why pull beats push

A **push** ad announces something and asks for attention:

> "Introducing our complete 12-module system! Everything you need to start your online business.
> Get instant access today!"

Nothing there is about the reader. It's a list of things the seller has. The reader's brain
correctly files it as an advertisement and the thumb keeps moving.

A **pull** ad describes the reader's own situation so precisely that stopping feels involuntary:

> "You've had the idea for about two years now. You've told maybe three people about it. It's
> still a note in your phone."

That isn't clever. It's *recognized*. And recognition is the only thing that reliably stops a
scroll, because it triggers a completely different reaction: **"how do they know that?"**

**The mechanism:** people don't buy when they understand your product. They buy when they feel
understood. Understanding flows one direction — you demonstrate that you understand *them*, and
they extend trust in return. Push ads ask for trust before earning it. Pull ads earn it in the
first line.

### The practical test

Read your first line. If it could appear in a competitor's ad with the product name swapped,
it's a push line. Rewrite it.

Second test: does the first line contain the product? If yes, you've almost certainly started in
the wrong place. **The product should arrive late** — after the reader has already agreed with
you about their own situation.

## 2. The anatomy of a pull ad

Five beats, in this order. The order is the strategy.

**1. The moment (1–2 lines).** A specific, ordinary, slightly uncomfortable scene the reader
recognizes. Not a problem *stated* — a moment *shown*. Not "you're overwhelmed" but "it's 10pm
and you've just opened the laptop again."

**2. The turn.** Name the thing they've quietly concluded about themselves — and let them off the
hook. This is the most powerful beat in the whole ad and the one most people skip. *"That's not a
discipline problem. It's what happens when you're handed a to-do list at the exact moment you
have nothing left to give it."*

**3. The reframe.** Give them a new way to see it. The old explanation was wrong, and here's the
structural reason. This is where you become useful rather than promotional.

**4. The resolution.** *Now* the product — but described as the answer to the specific thing you
just named, not as a feature list. One or two concrete capabilities, chosen because they resolve
*that* tension.

**5. The invitation.** One action. Low pressure. Stated once. *"Have a look — that's all."*

### What to cut

- Adjective stacks — "powerful, comprehensive, all-in-one." They convey nothing and signal ad.
- Anything the reader hasn't earned yet. No price, no urgency, no guarantee in the first three
  lines. Those answer objections the reader hasn't formed.
- Exclamation marks past the first one.
- "Imagine…" as an opener. It asks for effort before giving anything.

## 3. Hook patterns that stop the scroll

The first line does 80% of the work. These patterns work because each creates an open loop the
brain wants closed.

**The unfinished admission** — implies vulnerability, invites completion.
> "Nobody tells you the part that actually stops people."

**The specific scene** — recognition, not description.
> "Your laptop is open. The doc is blank. It's been blank for twenty minutes."

**The permission** — releases a tension they're already holding.
> "You're allowed to start before you feel ready. In fact it's the only way it ever happens."

**The corrected assumption** — challenges a belief they hold, gently.
> "'Not technical enough' stopped being a real reason about two years ago."

**The quiet cost** — makes an invisible price visible.
> "Waiting doesn't feel expensive. That's exactly why it's so easy to keep doing."

**The named objection** — says their private thought out loud before they can.
> "You've bought something like this before, and nothing happened."

**The two versions** — contrast that does the persuading for you.
> "Two people start the same month with the same idea. Five years later they're not close."

**The honest disqualifier** — signals confidence, filters, and builds trust simultaneously.
> "If you want a button that prints money, this isn't it."

### Hooks to avoid

- "Attention [group]!" — reads as spam.
- "Are you tired of…" — the most-used opener in the category; invisible now.
- "I was just like you until…" — only works with a real, specific story attached.
- Anything with a fake-urgency countdown. It buys a short-term click and costs long-term trust.

## 4. The angle library

Six or eight *rewordings* of one idea will all lose together. Distinct **angles** fail
independently — which is what actually finds a winner. Each of these attacks a different
psychological barrier, so run several and let the data choose.

| Angle | The barrier it removes | Where it starts |
|---|---|---|
| **Capability** | "Someone like me can't do this" | Who it worked for, and why that's the point |
| **Prior failure** | "I've tried, nothing happened" | Naming the last disappointment honestly |
| **Time** | "I don't have hours to spare" | The realistic small version |
| **Invisibility** | "I don't want to be on camera" | Faceless as the advantage, not the compromise |
| **Ownership** | "I don't want another subscription" | Renting vs owning, five years out |
| **The cost of waiting** | "I'll get to it later" | The year that already passed |
| **Proof/demonstration** | "I don't believe it works" | Show the thing working, say almost nothing |
| **Identity** | "This isn't who I am" | The version of them who already did it |

**Name the angle** in the ad's name so that when one wins, they learn which *idea* won — not just
which wording. That's the difference between a lucky ad and a repeatable insight.

## 5. Storytelling in a space this small

You have about three seconds and forty words before the reader decides. Full narrative arcs don't
fit. These do:

**The single scene.** One specific moment, told in present tense. Specificity is the whole trick
— "3am, chest tight, doing the math again" lands where "financial stress" doesn't. Concrete
detail is what makes a reader supply their own version of the memory.

**The two-timelines.** Same person, one decision apart. Lets the reader place themselves without
being told what to feel.

**The confession.** Something true and slightly costly to admit. Instantly separates you from
every polished ad around it. Only ever use a real one — a fabricated confession is detectable and
unrecoverable.

**The overheard line.** A sentence someone actually said that changed something. Works because
dialogue feels found rather than written.

### Rules for using a real story

- **Specific beats dramatic.** The small true detail outperforms the big vague claim.
- **Never invent it.** If it didn't happen, don't write it. One fabricated story, discovered,
  costs more than every ad it ever ran in.
- **Point it at them, not at you.** The story is a mirror, not a memoir. End on their situation.
- **Respect stated boundaries.** If there's a word or a detail the owner won't publish, that rule
  is absolute — find another way to tell it.

## 6. Claim-safe language

This is not optional. Income claims get ad accounts banned, and they're dishonest. There is
always a stronger honest version.

| ❌ Never | ✅ Instead |
|---|---|
| "Make $5,000/month" | "Build something that's yours" |
| "Replace your salary" | "Stop trading every hour for every dollar" |
| "Guaranteed results" | "A 30-day guarantee, so the risk is mine" |
| "Passive income" | "Work you do once that keeps working" |
| "I made $X — you can too" | "Here's exactly how it's built. You decide." |
| "Quit your job" | "Options you don't currently have" |

**Also avoid:** implying you know anything about the reader's personal circumstances (Meta's
personal-attributes policy), before/after financial screenshots, and any countdown that isn't
genuinely real.

**Costs and time are fair game.** "Under $30/month in tools" or "about 30 minutes a day" are
factual and allowed. It's *earnings* that are prohibited — and the honest version is usually the
better-converting one anyway, because it doesn't trip the reader's skepticism.

## 7. Reading the numbers

### The floor before judging anything

**No verdict under ~$20 spend or 3 days.** Early numbers swing wildly and mean nothing. Killing a
good ad after $6 because it hadn't converted yet is the most common and most expensive habit in
paid social. Say "not enough data yet" and mean it — that answer is worth more than a guess.

### What each number is actually telling you

| Signal | Reading | Move |
|---|---|---|
| Spend, zero conversions, past the floor | Genuinely not working | Turn it off |
| Cost per result above break-even | Losing money per sale | Turn it off |
| Cost per result comfortably under target, 3+ conversions | Working | Raise budget ~20–25%, no more |
| **Frequency above ~2.5** | Same people seeing it repeatedly | **New creative** — not new targeting |
| Good CTR, poor conversion | The ad works, the page doesn't | Fix the page, keep the ad |
| Poor CTR, good conversion when they do land | The offer works, the hook doesn't | New hook, same offer |
| Everything drops at once | Usually creative fatigue or an audit | Refresh creative before panicking |

**Frequency is the most under-read number.** When performance decays it's almost always because
the same people have seen the ad too many times — not because targeting broke. Refresh the hook
first; changing the audience resets learning for nothing.

### Scaling without breaking it

- Raise budget in **~20–25% steps** (the monitor suggests 20%), and wait 3–4 days between steps. Doubling a budget usually
  resets the learning phase and performance gets worse before it recovers.
- **Scale the ad set, not the ad.** Duplicating a winning ad into a new campaign rarely
  reproduces it — the learning lived in the ad set.
- **Never edit a winner.** Editing creative or targeting on a performing ad can reset it. Build
  the variation as a new ad alongside it.

### The number nobody tracks and everybody needs

**Value per lead over 30–60 days.** Cost per lead alone tells you nothing about whether a campaign
is good. A $12 lead is excellent if a lead is worth $40 and terrible if it's worth $5.

Once that number is known, budget stops being a guess and becomes arithmetic — spend up to the
value, scale until the auction stops cooperating. It's also the number that decides whether a
higher-priced direct-sales campaign is viable yet. Instrument it early with UTMs on the contact
record; everything else in ads is downstream of it.

## 8. The five most expensive mistakes

1. **A new campaign every week.** Permanent learning phase. Add creative to the existing ad set.
2. **Judging on thin data.** Killing winners early, keeping losers on a fluke good day.
3. **Optimizing for an event the pixel never fires.** Spends fully, learns nothing, delivers junk.
4. **Chasing a cheap cost-per-lead into the wrong countries.** The metric improves, the business
   doesn't, and the algorithm learns the wrong lesson.
5. **Six variations of one idea.** They lose together and teach nothing. Vary the *angle*.

---

## 9. The arithmetic that sets the budget

**Your maximum cost per lead = what a lead is worth in its first 30 days ÷ the return you need.**

Example: 100 leads bring 3 sales of a $47 product in 30 days → each lead is worth about $1.41. If
you want every ad dollar back at least 1.5× (so you can cover tools and still grow), your ceiling is
about $0.94 per lead. Above it, you're paying to lose money; below it, you can scale.

Until the owner has 30 days of real numbers, say so plainly and use the guardrails in the monitor
(the spend floor and target cost) as a *starting* guess — then replace the guess with their real
value-per-lead as soon as it exists. A purchase campaign needs the same math with value per sale.

## 10. Lessons from real campaigns (small budgets, cold traffic, a digital product)

- **Cheap clicks + no sales = the page, not the ad.** If people click at a healthy rate (roughly 1%+
  link click-through) and nobody buys or signs up, the ad did its job. Switching it off throws away
  your best traffic. Check the page on a phone, the checkout, the load speed, and whether the page
  delivers what the ad promised. The monitor flags this as **CHECK YOUR PAGE** for that reason.
- **Proof beats pretty.** A short video that *shows the thing working* (the product, the result,
  the process) usually beats a polished static for a sale. Statics are fine for free lead magnets.
- **Put the payoff line first.** The same ad with its strongest line moved to the top often wins by
  a wide margin. Test openers before you test anything else.
- **Buyers come back.** On a 7-day click window, a sale often lands days after the click. Don't
  judge a sales ad on day 2.
- **3–5 live ads per ad set.** More than that splits a small budget so thin that nothing learns.
  Keep the rest paused on the bench and rotate a new one in when an ad fatigues.
- **Don't raise the budget on something that hasn't sold yet.** Scale what converts; fix what doesn't.
- **Test a free step first.** A free guide, checklist or workshop reaches the ~50 conversions a week
  learning needs on a small budget; a direct sale of a higher-priced product usually can't.

## 11. Before you hand over any ad — the checklist

Fix anything that fails before the owner sees it:

- [ ] **No income or earnings claims** — no "$X/month", "replace your salary", "passive income",
      "guaranteed", "get rich", before/after money screenshots. Costs and time saved are fine.
- [ ] **No personal-attribute callouts** ("Are you a broke mom?", "Struggling with debt?") — Meta
      rejects ads that imply they know the viewer's personal traits. Describe the situation instead.
- [ ] **No organic tricks in paid** — no "comment WORD below" (ads don't trigger comment automations
      reliably); use the button.
- [ ] **The ad promises only what the destination delivers** — if the free guide doesn't contain a
      "30-day plan", the ad can't mention one.
- [ ] **No "no catch / no pitch"** on a free thing that leads to an offer. Be honest about the next step.
- [ ] Each ad is a **different angle**, named, so the results teach something.
- [ ] Headline under ~40 characters; primary text opens on the viewer's moment, not the product.
- [ ] Image text is readable on a phone at thumbnail size.
